1.0Integers, the number line, and absolute value

DEFINITION

Integers are the collection of natural numbers, zero, and the negatives of natural numbers: {…, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, …}. Natural numbers (1, 2, 3, …) are also called positive integers; their opposites are negative integers. Zero is neither positive nor negative.

On the number line, every integer to the right is greater than every integer to its left. So … −3 < −2 < −1 < 0 < 1 < 2 < 3 … Two useful consequences: every positive integer is greater than every negative integer, and 0 is greater than every negative integer but less than every positive one. Also, the more negative an integer looks (the bigger the number after the minus sign), the smaller it actually is — −100 < −2.

DEFINITION — Absolute value

The absolute value of an integer a, written |a|, is its distance from 0 on the number line, regardless of direction. Distance is never negative, so |a| ≥ 0 always. |−7| = 7 and |7| = 7, but |0| = 0.

EXAMPLE 1 — absolute value and ordering
(i) Evaluate |−19| and −|19|. (ii) Arrange in ascending order: −6, 4, 0, −11, 8, −2
(i) |−19| = 19 (distance from 0, always positive). −|19| means "take the negative of |19|" = −19. These are opposites of each other, not the same thing.
(ii) Sort by position on the number line, most-left (smallest) first: −11, −6, −2, 0, 4, 8
EXAMPLE 2 — comparing using absolute value
Which is greater, −23 or −8? Answer using the number line, not just absolute value.
|−23| = 23 and |−8| = 8, so −23 is "more negative". On the number line −8 sits to the right of −23.
So −8 > −23, even though 23 > 8 as absolute values — for negative integers, the one with the smaller absolute value is greater.

1.1Addition of integers

RULE

Same sign: add the absolute values and keep the common sign. (−4) + (−7) = −11

Different signs: subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger, and take the sign of the integer with the greater absolute value. 9 + (−13) = −4

EXAMPLE 3
Add: (i) (−38) + (−15)  (ii) 47 + (−62)  (iii) (−29) + 45
(i) Same sign → add and keep sign: −(38 + 15) = −53
(ii) Different signs → 62 − 47 = 15, take sign of larger absolute value (−62): −15
(iii) Different signs → 45 − 29 = 16, take sign of larger absolute value (45): +16

Addition of integers has the same five properties you know from whole numbers, plus one new one:

  • Closure property: a + b is always an integer, for any integers a, b.
  • Commutative property: a + b = b + a
  • Associative property: (a + b) + c = a + (b + c)
  • Additive identity: a + 0 = a = 0 + a — adding 0 changes nothing.
  • Additive inverse: for every integer a, there is an integer −a with a + (−a) = 0. a and −a are called additive inverses (or opposites) of each other.
EXAMPLE 4 — using the additive inverse
(i) Write the additive inverse of 34 and of −51. (ii) Find the value of 68 + (−68) + 23.
(i) Additive inverse of 34 is −34. Additive inverse of −51 is 51.
(ii) 68 and −68 are additive inverses, so they cancel to 0: 0 + 23 = 23.

1.2Subtraction of integers

RULE

To subtract an integer b from an integer a, add the additive inverse of b to a: a − b = a + (−b). This turns every subtraction into an addition you already know how to do.

EXAMPLE 5
Subtract: (i) 12 − 19  (ii) (−8) − (−15)  (iii) (−20) − 7
(i) 12 + (−19) = −7
(ii) Additive inverse of −15 is 15, so (−8) + 15 = 7
(iii) Additive inverse of 7 is −7, so (−20) + (−7) = −27

Subtraction behaves very differently from addition — this trips up a lot of students, so RD Sharma is explicit about it:

  • Closure property: a − b is always an integer — subtraction is closed (unlike division, later).
  • NOT commutative: a − b ≠ b − a in general. e.g. 5 − 2 = 3 but 2 − 5 = −3.
  • NOT associative: a − (b − c) ≠ (a − b) − c in general. e.g. 8 − (5 − 2) = 5 but (8 − 5) − 2 = 1.
  • Property of zero: a − 0 = a, but 0 − a = −a (these are not the same unless a = 0).
EXAMPLE 6 — checking commutativity fails
Verify whether (−9) − 4 = 4 − (−9).
Left side: (−9) − 4 = (−9) + (−4) = −13
Right side: 4 − (−9) = 4 + 9 = 13
−13 ≠ 13, so subtraction of integers is not commutative — order matters.

1.3Multiplication of integers

RULE

The product of two integers of like signs is equal to the product of their absolute values (a positive result).

The product of two integers of opposite signs is equal to the additive inverse of the product of their absolute values (a negative result).

EXAMPLE 7 — Determine each product
(i) 12 × 7  (ii) (−15) × 8  (iii) (−25) × (−9)  (iv) 125 × (−8)
(i) Both positive → 12 × 7 = 84
(ii) Opposite signs → −(15 × 8) = −120
(iii) Both negative (like signs) → +(25 × 9) = 225
(iv) Opposite signs → −(125 × 8) = −1000
EXAMPLE 8 — Product of three or more integers
Find: (i) 3 × (−8) × 5  (ii) 9 × (−3) × (−6)  (iii) (−2) × (−4) × (−6) × (−8)
(i) 3 × (−8) = −24, then −24 × 5 = −120
(ii) 9 × (−3) = −27, then −27 × (−6) = 162
(iii) Four negative factors (even count) → product is positive: 2 × 4 × 6 × 8 = 384
  • An even number of negative factors in a product → the product is positive.
  • An odd number of negative factors → the product is negative.
EXAMPLE 9 — using properties to simplify
Find the value of: 1487 × 327 + (−487) × 327
Both terms share a factor of 327 — factor it out (distributive property):
327 × [1487 + (−487)]
= 327 × 1000
= 327000

1.4Properties of multiplication of integers

  • Closure property: for any integers a, b — a × b is always an integer.
  • Commutative property: a × b = b × a
  • Associative property: (a × b) × c = a × (b × c)
  • Distributive property (over addition): a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c
  • Multiplicative identity: a × 1 = a = 1 × a
  • Multiplication by zero: a × 0 = 0 = 0 × a
  • Multiplication by (−1): a × (−1) = −a = (−1) × a — multiplying by −1 just flips the sign.
EXAMPLE 10 — find the integer
Determine the integer whose product with (−1) is: (i) 58  (ii) 0  (iii) −225
Multiplying by −1 flips the sign, so work backwards by flipping again.
(i) The integer is −58, since (−58) × (−1) = 58
(ii) The integer is 0, since 0 × (−1) = 0
(iii) The integer is 225, since 225 × (−1) = −225

1.5Division of integers

RULE

Division undoes multiplication, so the sign rules are identical: like signs give a positive quotient, opposite signs give a negative quotient.

EXAMPLE 11
Divide: (i) 102 by 17  (ii) −85 by 5  (iii) 0 by (−135)
(i) 102 ÷ 17 = 6
(ii) Opposite signs → −(85 ÷ 5) = −17
(iii) 0 divided by any non-zero integer is always 0.

1.6Properties of division of integers

  • NOT closed: a ÷ b is not always an integer, e.g. 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5
  • NOT commutative: a ÷ b ≠ b ÷ a in general
  • NOT associative: a ÷ (b ÷ c) ≠ (a ÷ b) ÷ c in general
  • a ÷ 1 = a for every integer a
  • a ÷ a = 1 for every non-zero integer a
  • a ÷ 0 is never defined; 0 ÷ a = 0 for any non-zero a

1.7Order of operations — BODMAS

When an expression has multiple operations with no brackets, RD Sharma uses the BODMAS order strictly: Division and Multiplication (left to right, whichever comes first), then Addition and Subtraction (left to right).

EXAMPLE 12
Find the value of: 36 ÷ 6 + 3
Division first: 36 ÷ 6 = 6
Then addition: 6 + 3 = 9
EXAMPLE 13
Find the value of: (−3) × (−4) ÷ (−2) + (−1)
Division first (left to right): (−4) ÷ (−2) = 2
Then multiplication: (−3) × 2 = −6
Then addition: (−6) + (−1) = −7

1.8Simplifying expressions with brackets

RD Sharma introduces one extra symbol here: the vinculum — a bar drawn over a group of numbers, like 7‾−5‾. It works exactly like an invisible bracket: whatever is under the bar gets simplified before anything else, even before round brackets.

ORDER FOR REMOVING GROUPING SYMBOLS

Vinculum → small brackets ( ) → curly brackets { } → square brackets [ ] — and within each type, always work from the innermost bracket outward.

EXAMPLE 14
Simplify: 3 − (5 − 6 ÷ 3)
Inside the bracket, division comes first: 6 ÷ 3 = 2
3 − (5 − 2) = 3 − 3
= 0
EXAMPLE 15 — nested brackets, working outward
Simplify: 27 − [38 − {46 − (15 − 11)}]
Innermost first: (15 − 11) = 4
27 − [38 − {46 − 4}]
Curly bracket next: {46 − 4} = 42
27 − [38 − 42]
Square bracket: [38 − 42] = −4
27 − (−4) = 27 + 4 = 31
EXAMPLE 16 — with division inside nested brackets
Simplify: 45 − [38 − {60 ÷ 3 − (6 − 9 ÷ 3) ÷ 3}]
Innermost bracket: 9 ÷ 3 = 3, so (6 − 3) = 3
45 − [38 − {60 ÷ 3 − 3 ÷ 3}]
Do the divisions inside the curly bracket: 60 ÷ 3 = 20, and 3 ÷ 3 = 1
45 − [38 − {20 − 1}] = 45 − [38 − 19]
45 − 19 = 26

1.9Word problems with integers

The whole point of learning the rules is to use them on real situations — temperatures, money, floors below ground, depth underwater. The method is always the same: turn the story into a signed number sentence, then apply the rules from this chapter.

EXAMPLE 17 — temperature
At 6 a.m. the temperature in a hill town was −4°C. By noon it had risen by 9°C, and by midnight it had fallen by 12°C from the noon value. What was the temperature at midnight?
Noon temperature: −4 + 9 = 5°C
Midnight temperature: 5 − 12 = −7°C
EXAMPLE 18 — depth below sea level
A submarine is cruising at 180 m below sea level. It dives a further 45 m. Represent its new depth as an integer.
180 m below sea level = −180. Diving further means going more negative: −180 − 45 = −225
The submarine is now at −225 m, i.e. 225 m below sea level.

Chapter 1 summary

  • |a| is the distance of a from 0, always ≥ 0. On the number line, right is greater; for two negatives, the one closer to 0 is greater.
  • Addition: same sign → add and keep sign; different signs → subtract and take the sign of the larger absolute value. Addition is closed, commutative, associative, has identity 0, and every integer has an additive inverse.
  • Subtraction: a − b = a + (−b). Subtraction is closed but is NOT commutative and NOT associative.
  • Multiplication/division: like signs → positive result; opposite signs → negative result. Even count of negative factors → positive product; odd count → negative product.
  • Multiplication is closed, commutative, associative, and distributes over addition; 1 is the multiplicative identity; ×(−1) flips the sign.
  • Division is NOT closed, NOT commutative, NOT associative. Division by 0 is never defined.
  • BODMAS with no brackets: division/multiplication (left to right) before addition/subtraction (left to right).
  • With brackets: vinculum first, then ( ), then { }, then [ ] — innermost first within each.
